9  Exam preparation

How to use this page

This page is designed to help you prepare for the types of thinking required in the exam, not to rehearse the exam questions themselves.

  • The multiple-choice questions below check core terminology and decision-making.
  • The short-answer prompts ask you to practise writing concise, marker-friendly bullet points.
  • The applied scenarios are deliberately broad and do not include model answers. Use them to identify areas where you need to return to lectures, seminars, readings, or your own notes.

9.1 A quick note about short-answer responses

For short-answer questions, aim for clear, concise bullet points using the correct terminology. Markers reward discrete correct ideas, not long paragraphs.

For example:

In one or two points, explain why a coach might report the median rather than the mean for a squad’s soreness scores.

What we’re after
  • The median is less affected by extreme values/outliers than the mean.
  • It can better represent the typical response when the data are skewed.
What we’re not after

“The median is a useful statistic that sport scientists use in lots of ways. It is one of the main descriptive statistics and it helps coaches understand the group. It can be calculated from the data and then used to make better decisions.”

That answer is long but does not clearly state why the median is useful. Aim for the first box.

  • Validity and whether a test measures what it claims to measure
  • Meaningful change: TE, SWC, MDC, MCID
  • Agreement between measurement methods
  • Correlation, regression and prediction
  • Plotting data before analysis
  • Internal vs external load
  • Objective vs subjective monitoring tools
  • Dose-response thinking in athlete monitoring
  • Hypothesis testing, p-values, effect sizes and practical significance
  • Test selection, standardisation and applied decision-making
  • Lit Review feedback

    • Referencing and academic writing: Common issues included inconsistent reference lists, incorrect citation formatting, use of quotes throughout the review, and citation errors (e.g., first author names, multiple parentheses, full paper titles within text). Recommendation: carefully review referencing guidelines and proofread citation formatting prior to submission.

    • Historical/contextual development of the tool: Historical sections were often overly generic (e.g., “observation → HR → GPS”) without sufficient detail regarding the evolution of methods, technologies, timelines, or key studies. Recommendation: treat this section as a sourced historical timeline and discuss the development of the specific technology/tool in more depth and context.

    • Validity and reliability: This was generally the weakest section across many reviews despite representing a major component of the marking criteria. Many students relied on broad statements rather than reporting specific outcomes from primary studies (e.g., CV, TE, ICC, correlation coefficients). Recommendation: anchor claims to specific validation/reliability studies, report actual statistical outcomes, and prioritise deeper interpretation of one or two key papers rather than broad superficial summaries.

    • Depth over breadth: Stronger reviews tended to demonstrate genuine depth and understanding of a smaller number of studies, rather than superficially discussing many papers. Recommendation: focus on critically analysing a few highly relevant studies and clearly explain what they demonstrate regarding the monitoring tool.

    • Framework application and conceptual understanding: Many students referenced concepts such as external vs internal load without identifying the originating framework or meaningfully applying it throughout the review. Recommendation: introduce one relevant framework early and consistently return to it to help structure interpretation and discussion.

    • Balance across sections and marking criteria: Some reviews devoted substantial space to lower-weighted sections (e.g., conclusions/future directions) while providing minimal detail in highly weighted sections such as validity and reliability. Recommendation: align the depth and word allocation of sections with the assessment weighting and marking rubric.

    • Future directions and practical implications: Future directions were often broad, generic, or disconnected from the evidence discussed throughout the review. In some cases, students shifted toward entirely different technologies/tools in the conclusion. Recommendation: ensure future directions logically emerge from limitations, gaps, or findings identified within the review itself and remain directly related to the chosen monitoring tool.

11 Part B — Short-answer practice prompts

Use lecture notes to help cross reference your answers

11.1 1. Describing a squad’s data

A practitioner reports two groups’ isometric mid-thigh pull scores:

  • Group A: 2100 ± 180 N
  • Group B: 1980 ± 320 N

Write 3–4 bullet points explaining what the mean and SD tell you about the two groups. Include one limitation of interpreting only the mean and SD.

11.2 2. Choosing summary statistics

A wellness score is collected from 20 athletes. Most scores are clustered between 6 and 8, but one athlete reports a score of 1.

  • Would your report the mean or median here and why?
  • What measure of spread might help communicate the variability?
  • What would plotting the scores add before you summarise them?

11.3 3. Classifying monitoring metrics

Classify each metric as external load, objective internal load, or subjective internal load. Briefly justify each choice.

  • PlayerLoad from an accelerometer
  • Blood lactate after a repeated-sprint session
  • Session-RPE
  • Morning muscle soreness rating
  • Mean heart rate during a small-sided game

11.4 4. Validity types

Choose three types of validity from the list below. Define each one and give a sport-science example that is not copied from the wording of your notes.

  • Face validity
  • Content validity
  • Construct validity
  • Concurrent validity
  • Predictive validity
  • Ecological validity

11.5 5. Reliability and standardisation

A new change-of-direction test is being trialled with academy athletes.

List five sources of unwanted variation that could reduce reliability. For each one, suggest one practical way to standardise or control it.

11.6 6. Meaningful change

An athlete improves their jump score by an amount that is larger than the SWC but smaller than the MDC.

In 3 bullet points, explain how you would interpret that result for a coach. Use the terms practically meaningful, measurement noise, and confidence.

11.7 7. Agreement between devices

A low-cost wearable gives similar group averages to a laboratory device but individual readings sometimes differ substantially.

  • Why is the group bias not enough to decide whether the devices can be used interchangeably?
  • What do limits of agreement add?
  • Why might a high correlation still be insufficient evidence of agreement?

11.8 8. Correlation and prediction

A relationship between a field test and match output is reported as r = 0.35, p < 0.05.

  • What does the p-value suggest?
  • What does the size of r suggest?
  • Would you be cautious about using the field test to predict individual match output? Why?

11.9 9. Internal load scales

Compare a within-session RPE scale, session-RPE, and Borg RPE approach.

  • What is each trying to capture?
  • Why does timing of administration matter?
  • What are two practical advantages and two limitations of subjective scales?

11.10 10. Biomarkers in applied monitoring

Choose one hormonal, immune, or biochemical marker discussed in the unit.

  • What does it broadly reflect physiologically?
  • What is one advantage of using it?
  • What are two limitations in an applied sport environment?
  • Why should it usually be interpreted alongside other monitoring information?

11.11 11. Plotting before analysis

List five things a plot can help you check before running or interpreting statistical analyses. For each one, write a phrase explaining why it matters.

11.12 12. Planning a field test

You have been asked to develop a new test for a team sport. Write the first five planning steps you would take before collecting data. Focus on the logic of the process rather than memorising a fixed list.

12 Part C — Applied scenario practice

The following scenarios are designed to make you practise decision-making. They do not include solutions because the aim is to rehearse reasoning, not memorise answers.

12.1 Scenario 1 — Selecting a monitoring test

A volleyball program is deciding between two lower-body monitoring tests:

Test CV% Typical error Relationship with match-relevant outcome
Test A: controlled jump test 3.5% low moderate relationship with match jumping actions
Test B: reactive movement task 6.8% higher large relationship with match movement demands

Discuss:

  • What trade-off exists between reliability and validity here?
  • Which test might you prioritise for routine monitoring, and under what conditions?
  • Could there be a reason to keep both tests?
  • What additional information would help you make the decision?

12.2 Scenario 2 — Tracking change over time

A swimmer’s weekly neuromuscular score varies slightly from week to week during heavy training, then increases noticeably after a taper.

Discuss:

  • What information about the test do you need before calling the increase a real change?
  • How would TE, SWC, MDC and MCID each help your interpretation?
  • How would you explain the difference between a detectable change and a worthwhile change to a coach?
  • What would you do if the athlete’s change is promising but still within the test’s measurement noise?

12.3 Scenario 3 — Comparing a cheaper device

A team compares a low-cost timing app with its existing timing gates across repeated acceleration trials. The app is cheaper and easier to set up, but the differences between the two systems are not the same for every athlete.

Discuss:

  • What does systematic bias tell you?
  • What do limits of agreement tell you that the bias does not?
  • Why should the limits of agreement be considered relative to the smallest worthwhile change?
  • Would you be comfortable switching devices halfway through a season? Why or why not?

12.4 Scenario 4 — Same external load, different response

Two athletes complete the same conditioning session. Their external load metrics are almost identical, but Athlete 1 reports low session-RPE and normal recovery markers, whereas Athlete 2 reports high session-RPE, poor sleep and elevated heart-rate response.

Discuss:

  • Which information reflects the external dose?
  • Which information reflects the internal response?
  • What could explain the mismatch between the two athletes?
  • How might this affect the next day’s training decision?
  • Why is it risky to rely on only one monitoring measure?

12.5 Scenario 5 — Hypothesis test

A study reports that a fitness test is statistically significantly related to match running output. The relationship is positive but modest.

Discuss:

  • What does statistical significance tell you?
  • What does it leave out?
  • Why does the size of the relationship matter for prediction?
  • What other information would you want before recommending the test to a coach?

12.6 Scenario 6 — Standardising a test battery

A team introduces a preseason testing battery involving jump, sprint and wellness measures. Testing is completed by different staff members across two weeks.

Discuss:

  • What aspects of the protocol should be standardised?
  • Why do warm-up, instructions, timing, equipment setup and tester training matter?
  • How could inconsistent procedures affect reliability and validity?
  • What would you include in a written testing protocol?
