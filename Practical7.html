7  Week 11 - Practical

7.1 Overview

This practical covers the foundational assessment skills expected of an Accredited Exercise Scientist (AES). You will practise pre-exercise screening, conduct observational postural assessment, measure static flexibility using a battery of common tests, and explore dynamic movement evaluation. By the end of the session you should be able to justify your assessment choices, articulate what you observe, and use findings to inform exercise prescription – all within the ESSA AES scope of practice.

7.2 Part 1: Pre-Exercise Screening

Administer the Adult Pre-exercise Screening System (APSS) on a partner.

Stage 1 (compulsory): 7-item health questionnaire. Any “yes” response means the client should seek medical guidance before commencing exercise.

Stage 2 (recommended): Measured risk factors – resting heart rate, resting blood pressure, and relevant behavioural information to build a risk profile.

Use this as more than a form-filling exercise. It is your first conversation with the client. Ask open-ended questions: current activity levels, injury history, goals, and any concerns. Practise explaining what you are doing and why in plain language.

Key points:

  • Pre-screening is a legal duty of care and a professional obligation for ESSA-accredited practitioners.
  • An AES screens and assesses – they do not diagnose. Know when to refer and to whom (AEP, GP, specialist).
  • The APSS is the nationally recognised standard, co-developed by ESSA, AUSactive, and SMA.

7.3 Part 2: Postural Observation

PLEASE SEE ESSA STUDENT MANUAL BOOK CHAPTER 8. “Static and Dynamic Posture” for more information.

DEFINITIONS

  • Anatomical position: the erect position of the body with the face directed forwards, the arms at the side of the body, toes and palms of the hands facing forwards. Used as a reference to describe the relationship of body segments to one another.

  • Centre of mass: a hypothetical point around which the force of gravity appears to act and at which the combined mass of the body appears to be concentrated. In humans in the anatomical position, it is approximately anterior to the second sacral vertebra. The location changes with every new position of the body and limbs.

  • Base of support (BoS): refers to the area beneath the person that is in contact with the supporting surface (i.e. in standing it is the feet and any other device in contact with the ground, e.g. crutches or walking sticks). It will vary in different positions (i.e. in sitting it would also include the chair).

  • Dynamic posture: the alignment of the body segments during movement.

  • Grid rating frame: a frame designed to provide visual cues of an individual’s posture to aid in correcting deviations.

  • Inclinometer: a device for measuring the angle of inclination of a body part.

  • Plumb line: a line (e.g. string) with a weight at one end (e.g. plumb bob) that is used to determine verticality.

  • Kinaesthetic: a conscious awareness of body position, muscle movement and mass as felt through nerve endings.

  • Kyphosis: excessive outward curvature of the spine.

  • Lordosis: excessive inward curvature of the spine.

  • Anterior pelvic tilt: the top of the pelvis rotates forward, increasing lumbar lordosis (looks like the pelvis “tipping down” at the front).

  • Posterior pelvic tilt: the top of the pelvis rotates backward, flattening lumbar lordosis (looks like the pelvis “tucking under”).

  • Genu recurvatum: hyperextension of the knee beyond neutral (looks like the knees “pushing backwards”).

  • Postural malalignment: differences in alignment between the left and right sides of the body and/or the front and back of the body.

  • Postural sway: horizontal movement around the centre of gravity.

  • Scoliosis: excessive lateral curvature of the spine.

  • Static posture: the alignment of the body while stationary.

  • Valgus: The part of the limb below the joint moves outward, making the joint look like it’s collapsing inward (knock‑kneed).

  • Varus: The part of the limb below the joint moves inward, making the joint look like it’s bowing outward (bow‑legged).

Observe your partner in standing from the front, back, and side.

Your task is to describe what you see, not to diagnose. Use specific, observable language rather than clinical labels. For example:

Instead of… Try…
“Excessive kyphosis” “The thoracic curve appears more pronounced than typical when viewed from the side”
“Anterior pelvic tilt” “The pelvis appears to be tilted forward relative to the trunk, with the lower back showing a more pronounced curve”
“Right upper trap hypertonia” “The right shoulder sits approximately 1-2 cm higher than the left”

What to look at:

  • Front: Are the shoulders level? Is the head centred? Any obvious knee valgus/varus?
  • Back: Shoulder and scapular symmetry. Any visible lateral spinal deviation? Are the iliac crests level?
  • Side: Head position relative to the shoulder. Thoracic and lumbar curvature. Knee position (hyperextended?).

Important caveats to remember:

  • There is enormous natural variation in posture across healthy, pain-free people. A “deviation” from a textbook ideal is not necessarily a problem.
  • The evidence linking static postural findings to pain or injury is weak and inconsistent.
  • Visual postural assessment has poor inter-rater reliability – two practitioners often disagree.
  • Beyond clearly significant findings (e.g., obvious scoliosis, major structural asymmetry), avoid over-interpreting. Your role as an AES is to notice and describe, not to diagnose.
  • If something concerns you, note it and refer appropriately.

Discussion: Compare your observations with a partner who assessed the same person. Where did you agree? Where did you disagree? What does that tell you about the reliability of this assessment method?

7.4 Part 3: Static Flexibility Assessment

Perform the following flexibility tests on your partner. For each test, record your findings and compare bilaterally where applicable.

7.4.1 3.1 Sit and Reach

  • Purpose: Combined hamstring and lower-back flexibility.
  • Protocol: Client sits with legs extended, feet flat against the box. They reach forward along the scale as far as possible, holding for 2 seconds. Record the best of 3 trials.
  • Equipment: Sit-and-reach box.
  • Normative reference: Males approximately 20-30 cm; Females approximately 23-33 cm (varies by protocol and age). Below approximately 15 cm is generally considered below average.
  • Watch for: Knees bending (invalidates the test), bouncing at end range, excessive thoracic rounding compensating for limited hamstring length.

7.4.2 3.2 Thomas Test

  • Purpose: Hip flexor length – specifically the one-joint hip flexors (iliopsoas).
  • Protocol: Client sits at the end of the plinth with their gluteal fold at the edge. They lie back, pulling both knees to the chest to flatten the lumbar spine, then release the test leg to hang freely.
  • Equipment: Plinth at appropriate height. Goniometer or inclinometer app optional.

Qualitative scoring:

  • Negative (normal): Test thigh rests in line with the table surface or the hip reaches neutral/slight extension with the pelvis controlled.
  • Positive (restricted): Test thigh lifts off the table or the hip remains flexed above horizontal.
  • Compensations to note: Lumbar extension (back arching off table), anterior pelvic tilt, thigh abduction or external rotation.
  • Compare left vs. right.

Video: Thomas test https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NMDd-4NspHs

7.4.3 3.3 Kendall Test (Modified Thomas for Rectus Femoris)

  • Purpose: Rectus femoris (two-joint hip flexor) length. This is performed in conjunction with the Thomas test to differentiate between one-joint and two-joint hip flexor tightness.
  • Protocol: In the same modified Thomas position, observe the degree of knee flexion in the hanging test leg. A tight rectus femoris will limit knee flexion because it is stretched across both the hip and knee joints simultaneously.
  • Equipment: Same setup as the Thomas test.

Qualitative scoring:

  • Negative (normal): The hanging knee remains flexed, commonly around 80-90 degrees.
  • Positive (restricted): The test-side knee extends (straightens) as the hip is held in the modified Thomas position, suggesting rectus femoris tightness.
  • Compare left vs. right. Often performed and reported alongside the Thomas test as a combined assessment.

Video: Kendall test https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n8zRF56N-U8

7.4.4 3.4 Back Scratch Test

  • Purpose: Combined shoulder flexion/external rotation (reaching over) and extension/internal rotation (reaching behind).
  • Protocol: Client reaches one hand over the same shoulder and the other hand behind the back from below. Measure the distance between the middle fingertips. Positive score means fingers overlap; negative score means there is a gap.
  • Equipment: Tape measure or ruler.
  • Normative reference: Males approximately -5 to 0 cm; Females approximately 0 to +3 cm. Asymmetry greater than 3-4 cm between sides is notable.

Qualitative scoring:

  • Good: Fingers overlap.
  • Neutral: Fingers touch.
  • Limited: Gap between fingers.
  • Compare sides and note any pain or compensation (particularly trunk lateral flexion to close the gap). Note which component is more limited – the overhead reach (flexion/external rotation) or the behind-back reach (extension/internal rotation).

Video: Back scratch test https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=60hR4F4cjic

7.4.5 3.5 Weight-Bearing Lunge Test (Knee-to-Wall)

  • Purpose: Weight-bearing ankle dorsiflexion range of motion.
  • Protocol: Client places their foot a set distance from a wall and lunges the knee forward toward the wall, keeping the heel on the ground. Measure the maximum distance from the great toe to the wall (cm) at the point where the knee can just touch the wall with the heel still down.
  • Equipment: Wall, tape measure.
  • Normative reference: Approximately 10-12 cm toe-to-wall distance. Side-to-side difference greater than 1.5-2 cm is notable and very common post-ankle sprain.

Qualitative scoring:

  • Pass at a given distance: Knee reaches the wall without heel lift, foot collapse, or knee valgus.
  • Restricted: Heel lifts before the knee reaches the wall, knee cannot touch the wall at a reasonable distance, pain occurs, or there is a large asymmetry between sides.
  • Ask the client whether they feel a “bony block” at the front of the ankle versus a muscular stretch in the calf – these suggest different limiting structures.

Video: WBLT https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U7woPNLUT3Q

7.4.6 3.6 Passive Straight Leg Raise

  • Purpose: Hamstring length. Also has a neurodynamic component if symptoms are reproduced.
  • Protocol: Client lies supine with both legs extended. The tester passively raises one leg (keeping the knee extended) until firm resistance is felt or the pelvis begins to rotate posteriorly.
  • Equipment: Goniometer or inclinometer app for precise measurement. If neither is available, use the subjective scoring scale below.
  • Normative reference: Approximately 70-90 degrees of hip flexion with knee extended. Below 70 degrees is generally considered limited.

Qualitative scoring:

  • Flexibility context: Restricted if there is lower ROM or clear asymmetry between sides.
  • Neurodynamic context: Positive if familiar buttock, leg, or radicular pain is reproduced – not just a hamstring stretch sensation.
  • Watch for: Pelvic rotation (mark where rotation begins – this is the true endpoint, not the forced maximum), contralateral leg lifting, knee bending. Note whether the stretch sensation is in the posterior thigh (hamstrings) or behind the knee (may indicate neural tension). Compare bilaterally.

Subjective scoring scale (if no goniometer/inclinometer available):

Score Description
1 - Limited Leg does not reach 45 degrees from horizontal. Notably restricted hamstring length.
2 - Moderate Leg reaches approximately 45-70 degrees from horizontal. Some limitation present.
3 - Normal Leg reaches approximately 70-90 degrees from horizontal. Within expected range.
4 - Above average Leg easily exceeds 90 degrees (vertical) with no pelvic compensation.

Video: PSLR test https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9ulTens_-7Q

7.4.7 3.7 MHFAKE Test (Maximal Hip Flexion Active Knee Extension)

  • Purpose: Hamstring length assessed under maximal hip flexion – more sport-specific than the passive SLR, commonly used in running and team-sport settings for hamstring assessment and return-to-sport monitoring.
  • Protocol: Client lies supine and pulls one hip into maximal flexion using their hands (thigh to chest). From this position, they actively extend the knee as far as possible. The angle of knee extension deficit from full extension (0 degrees) is measured.
  • Equipment: Goniometer or inclinometer app placed on the shin. If neither is available, use the subjective scoring scale below.
  • Normative reference: The closer to 0 degrees (full extension), the better the hamstring flexibility. Greater than 20-25 degrees deficit is generally considered limited.

Qualitative scoring:

  • Compare left and right – bilateral comparison is particularly important for this test.
  • Note pain, stretch tolerance, apprehension, or pelvic/lumbar compensation.
  • Mark as restricted if there is a clear side-to-side loss of range or if extension is pain-limited.
  • Watch for: Client losing maximal hip flexion during the knee extension (the hip must stay fully flexed throughout), contralateral leg lifting, and the location of the stretch sensation – posterior thigh (hamstrings) vs. behind the knee (neural tension).

Subjective scoring scale (if no goniometer/inclinometer available):

Score Description
1 - Limited Knee remains notably bent (greater than 30 degrees from full extension). Shin clearly angled away from vertical.
2 - Moderate Knee approaches but does not reach full extension (approximately 15-30 degree deficit).
3 - Normal Knee reaches within approximately 10-15 degrees of full extension. Shin is close to vertical.
4 - Above average Knee reaches full or near-full extension (less than 10 degree deficit) with hip held in maximal flexion).

Video: MHFAKE Test https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KEMj1oYGFEg

7.4.8 Measurement Tools: Inclinometer vs. Goniometer

A goniometer measures the angle between two body segments at a joint. A plastic goniometer has two arms you align along each segment, with the pivot over the joint axis.

An inclinometer measures the angle of a single segment relative to gravity (vertical or horizontal). Your phone is essentially an inclinometer – its accelerometer detects tilt.

For the passive SLR and MHFAKE, either tool works: the inclinometer app placed on the shin gives you the angle directly. For the Thomas test, an inclinometer on the thigh tells you whether it is above or below horizontal.

If you do not have a goniometer or inclinometer, the subjective scoring scales above give you a practical alternative – and comparing your subjective scores against a partner who used a goniometer on the same person makes for a useful discussion about measurement precision.

7.5 Part 4: Dynamic Movement Assessment

7.5.1 Design Challenge

Rather than following a prescribed screening protocol, your task is to design a short movement screening suitable for an apparently healthy adult. Select 3-5 dynamic movements and, for each one, specify:

  1. What you are assessing (the purpose)
  2. What you are looking for (specific observation criteria)
  3. How you will record your findings (scoring or descriptive)

7.5.2 Key Principles

  • Every movement you include should have a clear purpose. “Do a squat” is not enough – you need to define what you are observing.
  • Include movements across different planes (sagittal, frontal, transverse). A battery of only squats and lunges will miss lateral stability and rotational control.
  • Keep it practical – if it takes 30 minutes and requires specialised equipment, it will not get used.
  • Think about what is most useful for informing exercise prescription rather than generating a single composite score.

7.5.3 Example Movements to Consider

Overhead squat: Bilateral lower limb mobility and stability, trunk control, upper body mobility. Look at: depth, trunk angle, heel position, knee tracking, arm position. Consider: performance can be limited by ankle dorsiflexion, hip structure, or unfamiliarity rather than a meaningful dysfunction.

Single-leg squat: Unilateral lower limb control, particularly frontal plane hip-knee-ankle alignment. Look at: pelvic drop (Trendelenburg), knee valgus, trunk lean, depth and control. Consider: this is a challenging task – poor performance may reflect lack of practice rather than pathology.

Video: Single-leg squat https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y-v4seZ7eZ8

Push-up or trunk stability assessment: Trunk stability under load. Look at: ability to maintain a neutral spine, excessive lumbar extension or hip sag.

Single-leg balance or modified star excursion: Balance, proprioception, lower limb stability. Look at: reach distance in multiple directions, stability of the stance leg, compensatory trunk movements.

Video: Y - balance test https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qFVuS-P2FrI

CMJ: Whilst different cues exist, one common strategy is to coue to jump as heigh as you can as fast you can. Look for differences in movement strategies across.

These are examples – you may choose others. The critical thing is that you can justify your choices and articulate what you are observing and why.

7.5.4 Activity

  1. In small groups, design your screening document.
  2. Administer it on a partner. A third person observes and gives feedback.
  3. Rotate roles.

Debrief: Was your scoring system practical in real time? How long did the screening take? Where was there the most disagreement between observers? What would you change?

7.6 Summary

  1. Pre-screening is non-negotiable. The APSS is the Australian industry standard. Use it as a conversation, not just a form.
  2. Describe posture, do not diagnose it. Use specific observational language. Be honest about the limitations of visual assessment and the narrow scope of an AES.
  3. Static flexibility tests give you quantifiable data. Use standardised protocols, compare bilaterally, and know the normative references.
  4. Dynamic movement assessment is more informative than static observation for exercise prescription. The key is knowing what you are looking for and why.
  5. Choose the right measurement tool for the job. Goniometer for angles between segments, inclinometer for angles relative to gravity, tape measure for distance-based tests.
